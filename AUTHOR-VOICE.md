# Author Voice — how Saad writes

Read this together with `STYLE.md` before drafting or polishing any chapter text. If the two conflict, `STYLE.md` wins: it holds this book's own rules on template, terminology, Vancouver referencing and brand names.

The guide is drawn from Saad's published writing: *Bioceramic in Dentistry* (Alsaif & Al Otaibi, Riyadh, 2026; ISBN 978-603-07-0945-8, endodontic chapters 1–4) and his review in Int J Mol Cell Med. 2025;14(4):1099-1109 (doi:10.22088/IJMCM.BUMS.14.4.1099). English is his second language. Claude polishes the English and keeps his voice, his structure and his claims.

## Signature moves (keep these)
1. **Evidence first, verdict second.** Give the claim and its source, then say plainly how strong the evidence is: "Reports … conflict, but consensus holds that …"; "differences attributed to technique and material brand".
2. **Conflicting studies side by side.** "higher in one study; similar in others; lower in another". Show the disagreement rather than hiding it.
3. **Precise numbers with their source.** "a mean of about 165 minutes", "85% vs 52% success". Use "about" or "roughly" only where the source is approximate.
4. **Define the target first.** Where it fits, open a topic with the requirements of the ideal material, workflow or device, credited to a recognised source, then judge the options against them.
5. **Classic-to-current arc.** Start with where the technique came from, then move to current systems.
6. **Clinical link.** Tie each property to a decision: "which suits it to …", "an advantage in aesthetic regions".
7. **Close sections with the evidence gap.** For example, "long-term randomised clinical trials are still needed". Be specific about what is missing; this complements the Key points box.

## Sentence habits
- Medium-to-long sentences. Use semicolons to join related findings and em dashes for lists or asides.
- Active verbs with a study or material as the subject: "Zirconia outperformed …", "Mangano et al. reported …".
- Define each abbreviation at first use, then use it consistently.
- British spelling, as already used in this book: colour, behaviour, analysed, stabilised.
- One hedge per claim ("may", "suggests"), never stacked.

## Avoid
- Repeating the same figure with different values in different sections. Check every repeated number against its source.
- Restating one limitation (for example, "small sample sizes") in every section. Say it once, then refer back to it.
- Translation-style wording; prefer the standard term in the dental literature.
- Promotional closing lines ("will transform dentistry"). End on a concrete next step instead.
- AI tells: "pivotal", "plays a crucial role", "delve", "landscape", "holds great promise", "it is evident that".

## When editing Saad's own text
1. Correct grammar, articles, prepositions and word order. Do not change his claims, structure or citations.
2. Never add a reference, number or finding he did not supply. Mark doubts as `[CHECK: …]`.
3. List the edits that change meaning so he can confirm them.
